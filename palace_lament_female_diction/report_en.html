Three questions drive the study. Do palace-lament poems (a male-dominated genre) and female poets differ systematically in wording? When women write on the palace/boudoir theme themselves, does their diction differ from male ventriloquism (ventriloquism vs. self-voice)? And within the female self-voice, how do documentary palace verse and lyrical lament diverge? The four layers below — diction, lexical fields, syntax, and function words — take these in turn.

I

Diction: allusive court vs. personal emotion

Distinctive terms are ranked by weighted log-odds ratio (informative Dirichlet prior = the whole Complete Tang Poems). Palace-lament concentrates on court exempla — 長門 (Changmen Palace), 昭陽, 長信, 金屋 (“golden house”), 團扇 (round fan / Ban Jieyu), 飛燕 (Zhao Feiyan), 君恩 (the lord’s favour). Female poets favour personal, literary registers — 郎 (my love), 儂 (I), 相思 (longing), 紅箋 (red note-paper), 詩, 吟.

Restricting both sides to the same lament mode — male ventriloquism vs. female boudoir lament — the difference is chiefly one of technique: men keep piling up allusion and palace space, women write more directly.

II

Lexical fields: a key correction after splitting the self-voice

Per-10k-character density across five palace-lament lexical fields. The most important finding is a self-correction. Treating all female self-voice as one would suggest “men’s laments are more emotional than women’s” — but that compares a lament group (ventriloquism) with a documentary one (Hua Rui’s palace verse). Once lament is set against lament (ventriloquism vs. lyric), the female lyric is in fact more saturated in grief and favour.

III

Syntax: poem form and interrogative mood

Classical Chinese has no reliable POS tagger, so we use structural features. Female poets clearly prefer the seven-syllable line (68%), whereas palace-lament is balanced; the lament modes (ventriloquism, lyric) have high interrogative rates while documentary verse barely asks — the rhetorical question is a syntactic marker of “lament”.

IV

Function words: ventriloquism as a petition to the lord (君)

Function-word classes, per 10k characters. The sharpest point: male ventriloquism has by far the highest second-person (君 “lord” / 爾 “you”) — 122 vs. 67 for female poets — so a ventriloquized palace lament is structurally a woman’s address to her superior. Female writing, by contrast, uses more sentence-final particles (兮 / 矣 / 乎 / 哉), a more overtly emotional register; the lyric lament is densest in adverbs and temporal words — the sense of time passing weighs heaviest there.

VI

Data, method & limitations

The corpus is the Complete Tang Poems, 900 juan (41,882 poems). The palace-lament list (carrying author gender) is matched to the corpus after title normalization (yuefu prefixes, first-line subtitles, variant characters), at ~89% (the 45 misses are mostly poems in un-crawled juan or authors absent from the corpus). Female poets = the list’s female authors ∪ canonical Tang women poets (Xue Tao 薛濤, Yu Xuanji 魚玄機, Li Ye 李冶, …). Distinctive terms use the Monroe et al. (2008) weighted log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior over the whole corpus, robust to raw frequency.

Statistical limits The female self-voice samples are small (lyric n≈11, documentary n≈4); distinctive-term z-scores are weak and field/syntax ratios are volatile, so conclusions there are suggestive. They are best treated as hypotheses for close reading and literary history, to be tested with more female-lament material and bootstrap confidence intervals.
